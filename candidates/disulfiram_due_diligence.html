Disulfiram for Maureen — Due Diligence Review

Prepared: 2026-08-07 Context: A physician outside her care team has written an Rx for disulfiram. This document is pre-start due diligence: chemistry, pharmacology, risks, patient-specific relevance, and monitoring strategy.

Status: Research memo. Not medical advice. Every actionable item below is framed as a question for the prescribing physician, not a recommendation to override her.

0. Bottom line up front

Three things matter more than everything else in this document:

  1. ⚠ The dose almost certainly isn’t 25/50 mg. US disulfiram is manufactured only as 250 mg and 500 mg tablets. There is no 25 mg or 50 mg product. “Start smaller, then go up” maps exactly onto the standard 250 mg → 500 mg titration. This is a 10× difference and it flips the entire risk profile — verify before the first dose.

  2. Her prior oxaliplatin exposure is the single biggest patient-specific risk. She has had oxaliplatin twice (FOLFOXIRI+bev, then FOLFOX+cetuximab+encorafenib). Disulfiram causes a dose-dependent axonal sensorimotor neuropathy that is rare below 250 mg/day and reported almost exclusively at ≥250 mg/day. That is additive with oxaliplatin neuropathy, which is often already present and can be permanent.

  3. The Rx appears to be disulfiram alone, but the preclinical effect depends on copper. Essentially all of the anticancer literature — including the March 2026 APC paper driving the current buzz — uses disulfiram + copper. Disulfiram monotherapy is the weaker arm in nearly every experiment. This needs an explicit decision, not a default.

  4. ⚠ Her ctDNA has been negative since 2025-12-05, so there is no headroom to demonstrate benefit. Undetectable cannot become more undetectable. The risks below are all measurable; the benefit, structurally, is not. See §8.3 — this is the finding that most changes the shape of the decision.

Overall assessment: at standard dose with proper monitoring this is a reasonable, reversible, low-cost thing to try in a setting with few good options — but the honest evidence grade for benefit is preclinical only, and the clinical track record across ~21 human trials is poor. Given point 4, this would be a bet taken on mechanism, entered as prophylaxis against recurrence — not a trial you can read out. The case for it rests on low cost and reversibility, not on strength of evidence, and not on any expectation of being able to tell whether it worked.

1. What the news actually said

The “metabolic way to treat certain cancers” story traces to a single preclinical paper:

Liang, T., et al. (2026). ALDH2 inhibition induces synthetic lethality in APC-deficient colorectal cancer via ROS/ASK1/JNK pathway. Genes & Diseases. DOI: 10.1016/j.gendis.2026.102057

The claim: APC-mutant colorectal cells run a higher baseline load of toxic aldehydes from lipid peroxidation. ALDH2 is the enzyme that clears them. Block ALDH2 → aldehydes accumulate → ROS spike → ASK1/JNK stress cascade → apoptosis. Normal cells, with intact APC, have headroom and are spared. That is a synthetic-lethal framing, and APC status is the proposed patient-selection biomarker.

Reported results: 50–70% reduction in cancer cell survival with disulfiram alone; near-complete with added copper; ~60% tumour shrinkage in APC-mutant mouse models without apparent normal-tissue harm.

What it is not:

  • Not a clinical trial. Cell lines, organoids, and mouse xenografts only.
  • Not a high-impact venue. Genes & Diseases is a legitimate peer-reviewed journal but not Nature/Cell/NEJM tier.
  • Not independently replicated yet.
  • The authors themselves flag “important differences between animal models and human pharmacology that must be resolved before the strategy can reach patients.”

Much of the amplification you likely saw came through secondary outlets (news-medical.net and aggregator blogs) rather than primary coverage. The absence of cautions you noticed is a property of the coverage, not of the drug. The cautions are real and well documented; they just don’t make headlines.

2. Chemistry and how it works

Disulfiram (tetraethylthiuram disulfide, “Antabuse”) is a small-molecule dithiocarbamate — two diethyldithiocarbamate (DDC) halves joined by a disulfide bond. It has been in clinical use since 1951. It is a promiscuous metal chelator, and essentially all of its biology flows from that.

Mechanism A — ALDH inhibition (the APC/ALDH2 story)

DETC-MeSO covalently and irreversibly inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase. Because the inhibition is covalent, enzyme activity only returns as new protein is synthesised — which is why the alcohol reaction persists up to 14 days after the last dose. This is also the mechanism the new APC paper exploits.

Mechanism B — CuET / NPL4-p97 (the better-established anticancer mechanism)

This is the mechanism from Skrott et al., Nature 2017 — the work that started the modern disulfiram-oncology field. DDC complexes with copper to form CuET, which preferentially accumulates in tumour tissue. CuET immobilises NPL4, the adaptor of the p97/VCP segregase — the cell’s central machine for pulling ubiquitinated proteins out of complexes and membranes for degradation. NPL4 aggregates, p97-dependent protein turnover collapses, the unfolded protein response fires, and the cell dies of proteotoxic stress.

Structural work (Nature Communications 2020) refined this: cupric ions released from CuET under oxidative conditions disrupt NPL4’s zinc-finger motifs, locking a conformational switch. That release is oxidation-dependent — which is elegant, because tumour tissue is already under oxidative stress, giving a degree of tumour selectivity.

Both mechanisms require copper. Mechanism B obviously so. Mechanism A is potentiated by it.

The central pharmacological problem for oncology: oral disulfiram has poor and erratic bioavailability, and is largely degraded before reaching systemic circulation. Achieved plasma CuET concentrations in humans sit well below the levels that produce the dramatic effects in cell culture. This — more than any failure of the underlying biology — is the leading explanation for why ~21 clinical trials have produced no breakthrough despite spectacular preclinical data. An enormous amount of current work is on nanoparticle and cyclodextrin formulations precisely to solve this.

Read this honestly: the mouse data may be real and the oral drug may still not reach the tumour at meaningful concentration in a human.

5. Risks and side effects

5.1 The alcohol reaction — absolute and non-negotiable

Acetaldehyde accumulation produces flushing, throbbing headache, nausea, violent vomiting, tachycardia, chest pain, profound hypotension, and — in severe cases — arrhythmias, cardiovascular collapse, MI, seizures, and death. Onset within ~10 minutes.

It persists up to 14 days after the last dose.

Hidden ethanol sources that catch people out:

  • ⚠ Her “Magic Mouthwash” (diphenhydramine-lidocaine-Maalox-nystatin) — many compounded formulations and diphenhydramine elixirs contain ic ethanol (up to ~14%). This is on her active med list. Verify the exact formulation with the pharmacy.
  • Liquid/elixir medications, many cough syrups
  • Mouthwash (Listerine ~21% ethanol), some toothpastes
  • Vanilla and other extracts; wine/beer in cooking (not fully cooked off); vinegars
  • Sauces, marinades, some desserts, kombucha
  • Hand sanitiser and alcohol-based topicals (absorption is possible with heavy use)
  • Some IV drug diluents — anaesthesia and ER staff must be told she is on disulfiram

Cardiac note: her LVEF is currently normal (67% biplane, Feb 2026, up from 59% in January), which is reassuring. But binimetinib carries a cardiomyopathy risk requiring ongoing echo surveillance, and a disulfiram-ethanol reaction causes profound vasodilation and hypotension. If LVEF drops on binimetinib, the consequences of an accidental exposure get materially worse. Worth linking these two surveillance streams.

5.3 Peripheral neuropathy — her highest-salience risk

  • Dose- and duration-dependent, large-fibre axonal sensorimotor pattern
  • Essentially all reported cases occur at ≥250 mg/day; rare below that
  • Onset typically 15 days to 6 months (most within 2 months)
  • Incidence roughly 1 in 15,000 patient-years overall
  • Usually reversible on discontinuation — but not always
  • Mechanism: axonal degeneration plus direct Schwann-cell/myelin toxicity

⚠ She has had oxaliplatin in two separate regimens. Oxaliplatin-induced peripheral neuropathy is cumulative, frequently persistent, and sometimes permanent. Layering a second axonal toxin on a nerve population already damaged by platinum is the risk I would weight most heavily at 250–500 mg/day.

This makes a documented baseline neuro exam essential — if she has existing numbness, tingling, or proprioceptive loss, that needs to be graded and written down before the first dose, or you will have no way to attribute any later change.

5.4 Other adverse effects

  • Neuropsychiatric: drowsiness, fatigue, headache; at 500 mg, reversible delirium and psychosis (notably reported in the glioblastoma trials at continuous 500 mg)
  • Optic neuritis — rare but reported. Note she is already on Maxitrol eye drops and binimetinib carries ocular toxicity (retinopathy/uveitis) warnings; any visual change needs prompt ophthalmology review with both drugs in the differential
  • Metallic/garlic aftertaste — very common, benign
  • Dermatologic: acneiform eruption, rash. ⚠ Confounded — she is already managing cetuximab acneiform rash with doxycycline and topical clindamycin
  • Contraindications: severe myocardial disease, coronary occlusion, psychosis, active alcohol use, existing significant liver disease

⚠ The encorafenib interaction is the subtle one worth raising explicitly

Disulfiram is a prodrug. It must be bioactivated to DETC-MeSO, and that step runs principally through CYP3A4/5. Encorafenib is “a relatively potent reversible inhibitor of CYP3A4 and also a time-dependent inhibitor of CYP3A4” (FDA label).

Plausible consequence: her BRAF inhibitor may substantially blunt the conversion of disulfiram into its active metabolite — reducing both the anticancer effect and, paradoxically, the alcohol-reaction intensity.

Two practical implications:

  1. Disulfiram may simply do less in her than in a patient not on encorafenib. If it’s going to be tried, this is a reason it might fail for pharmacological rather than biological reasons.
  2. Do not let a blunted alcohol reaction be interpreted as “she tolerates alcohol fine.” CYP3A4 inhibition is not reliable protection, and encorafenib dosing changes could restore full reaction intensity without warning.

I have not found this specific pair studied directly — I’m reasoning from each drug’s documented CYP profile. It’s a hypothesis worth putting to the prescribing physician or a pharmacist, not an established finding.

⚠ Corrected 2026-10-05; sources checked 2026-10-06 (#830). The callout’s premise is wrong for her.

6. ⚠ The dose question — resolve this first

You recalled “25 mg, then up to 50 mg.”

US disulfiram is manufactured only in 250 mg and 500 mg tablets. No 25 mg or 50 mg product exists.

Meanwhile the standard regimens are:

SettingRegimen
Alcohol-aversion (FDA label)500 mg/day × 1–2 weeks, then 250 mg/day maintenance
Cancer trials (most common)250 mg/day + copper gluconate 2–8 mg elemental Cu
Cancer trials (MTD)500 mg/day — but continuous use caused reversible delirium or neuropathy prompting discontinuation in some patients
Phase II alternative80 mg TID + copper gluconate 1.5 mg TID

“Start smaller to see if she tolerates it, then go up” is a precise description of the 250 → 500 mg titration. The most likely explanation is a dropped zero in recall.

Why this matters so much:

→ Call the pharmacy and read the label. This is a five-minute task and everything else depends on it.

7. The copper question

The Rx as described is disulfiram alone. But:

  • Mechanism B (CuET/NPL4) is copper — no copper, no CuET, no p97 inhibition
  • The March 2026 APC paper’s headline result — “nearly complete” kill — is the +copper arm
  • Essentially every modern trial pairs them: 250 mg disulfiram + 2–8 mg elemental copper as copper gluconate, with a Phase 1 dose-escalation study concluding 8 mg elemental copper/day as the projected optimum, well tolerated in solid tumours involving the liver with no DLTs

But copper is genuinely double-edged, and this deserves real caution rather than enthusiasm:

  • Copper is a well-documented driver of angiogenesis and metastasis (“cuproplasia”). Copper chelation — the opposite intervention — has itself been trialled as an anti-angiogenic cancer therapy.
  • There is specific evidence that copper improves disulfiram’s anti-angiogenic activity via the EGFR/Src/VEGF pathway in glioma — mechanistically interesting for her, since she is on cetuximab (anti-EGFR). Whether that’s synergistic or antagonistic in an EGFR-blocked patient is genuinely unknown.
  • Her copper-exporter expression (ATP7A/ATP7B) was very low in the 2024 specimen (8th/1st percentile), suggesting copper retention — which could amplify both the intended effect and any off-target copper toxicity.
  • ⚠ She takes ferrous gluconate; iron and copper compete for absorption. Doses would need separating.

This is exactly the kind of judgement call to hand to the prescribing physician rather than resolve at home. The question to put to her is not “should we add copper?” but “the preclinical effect you’re targeting is copper-dependent — is copper part of your plan, and if not, what mechanism are we expecting to engage?“

8.3 ⚠ The finding that actually changes the decision

Her ctDNA has been negative since 2025-12-05 (the last report on file is 2026-03-03), so there is no headroom in which disulfiram could demonstrate benefit. She is already at the floor of the most sensitive tumour-burden assay available.

The consequences are worth stating plainly:

  • You cannot show it helped. Undetectable cannot become more undetectable. The only observable movement is upward — i.e. the assay can register failure, never success.
  • You can still show it harmed. New neuropathy or rising LFTs in the 2–12 week window after starting, on an otherwise stable regimen, is attributable.
  • So the ledger is asymmetric in the unfavourable direction: the risks are fully measurable, the benefit is structurally unmeasurable.

This also reframes her clinical situation. Negative ctDNA since 2025-12-05, first on FOLFOX + cetuximab + encorafenib and then on cetuximab + encorafenib + binimetinib maintenance, is a deep, durable response. Adding a drug with real neuropathy and hepatotoxicity risk to someone in deep remission is a materially different proposition from adding it to someone visibly progressing — the first is prophylaxis against recurrence, and the endpoint of successful prophylaxis is “nothing happens,” which is unfalsifiable in a single patient.

None of this makes disulfiram wrong. Preventing recurrence is a legitimate goal, and BRAF V600E MSS mCRC recurs often enough that wanting to do something is entirely reasonable. But it should be entered with clear eyes: this would be a bet taken on mechanism, not a trial you can read out. That argues for weighting tolerability heavily and for pre-committing to a stopping rule, since “is it working?” will not answer itself.

  6. Imaging at its existing interval.

A pharmacodynamic option worth asking about: the Phase 1 disulfiram/copper trial used S-glutathionylation as a target-engagement biomarker — a direct readout of whether the drug is doing anything biochemically, independent of tumour response. Almost certainly not orderable clinically, but given that tumour-burden endpoints are floored, target engagement is the only category of evidence that could tell you the drug is doing anything at all. Worth one question.

8.5 ⚠ Confounding compounds the problem

Beyond the floored-endpoint issue, she is on active therapy — encorafenib + binimetinib

  • cetuximab. Even if there were headroom to show improvement, any change would be confounded between disulfiram, her existing regimen, and natural variation.

With n=1, no washout, and an endpoint already at its floor, “did it work?” is not an answerable question. That is not by itself a reason to skip it — but it is a reason to decide in advance what would make you stop, rather than deciding later while staring at ambiguous numbers.

9. Questions for the prescribing physician

Resolve before the first dose:

  1. ⚠ What is the exact prescribed dose? 25 mg and 50 mg tablets do not exist; the standard titration is 250 → 500 mg. Confirm from the label.
  2. ⚠ Is copper part of the plan? The mechanism being targeted is copper-dependent. If not, what mechanism is expected to engage?
  3. ⚠ Does her Magic Mouthwash contain ethanol? Pharmacy can confirm the formulation.
  4. ⚠ Can we get a baseline neuro exam documented, given two prior oxaliplatin regimens?
  5. ⚠ Confirm the current Signatera result. The whole risk/benefit framing below depends on whether undetectable is still true today.

Worth raising:

  1. ⚠ Given her ctDNA is undetectable, what would we expect to observe if disulfiram is working? If the honest answer is “nothing we can measure,” is that an acceptable basis for accepting neuropathy and hepatotoxicity risk — and if so, for how long? (This is the central question. Everything else is detail.)
  2. Encorafenib inhibits CYP3A4, which disulfiram needs for bioactivation — does that blunt the expected effect, and does it change how much to trust an absent alcohol reaction? ⚠ Corrected 2026-10-05 (#830): the current label calls encorafenib a strong CYP3A4 inducer, so the question becomes whether it speeds activation. The second half stands.
  3. LFT monitoring schedule — given additive hepatotoxicity with binimetinib/encorafenib, would every 2 weeks through the 2–12 week high-risk window be appropriate?
  4. CEA rose 2.6 → 4.8 through Sept 2025 while ctDNA went undetectable. How does she read that discordance, and is CEA worth continuing to follow at all? (Corrected 2026-10-05: there was no discordance. Her ctDNA was positive and rising through 2025-10-01, as her CEA was, and has been negative since 2025-12-05 (§8). Whether CEA is worth following still stands.)
  5. CA 19-9 on 2026-03-16 read 78 against 20–27 on every surrounding draw — real spike or lab/parse artifact? Worth confirming against the source report.
  6. Her tumour’s ALDH2 sits at the 31st–36th percentile for colorectal adenocarcinoma (data attached) — does that change her read on the ALDH2-dependency rationale?
  7. NPLOC4 is at the 86th–87th percentile across both specimens — is the CuET/NPL4 mechanism a better rationale here than the ALDH2 one?
  8. Stopping rules — what would constitute enough evidence of harm, or of futility, to discontinue? Given there is no efficacy readout, this needs deciding up front.

10. Balanced summary

Arguments for trying it:

  • She has the APC mutation that is the new paper’s own selection biomarker
  • NPLOC4 (the direct CuET target) is at the 86th–87th percentile in both her CRC specimens — a genuinely supportive, stable, patient-specific finding
  • ALDH1A1 rose to the 95th percentile in the 2025 specimen — an ALDH-high/stem-like drift that disulfiram also targets
  • 75 years of human safety data; risks are known, monitorable, and largely reversible
  • Inexpensive; her oncologist is on board; readily stopped
  • Baseline LFTs are excellent, giving clean signal to monitor against
  • BRAF V600E MSS mCRC is a hard subtype with limited options — the calculus for a low-cost, reversible addition is different than it would be in a curable setting

Arguments for caution:

  • Evidence for benefit is preclinical only. No completed trial supports use in CRC.
  • ~21 human trials across many tumour types have produced no breakthrough, most likely because oral disulfiram cannot achieve tumour CuET concentrations that work in a dish
  • Her ALDH2 is below median — no positive enrichment on the specific target the news story is about
  • Two prior oxaliplatin regimens make additive neuropathy the standout risk at 250–500 mg
  • Additive hepatotoxicity with binimetinib + encorafenib + scheduled acetaminophen
  • Encorafenib may blunt disulfiram bioactivation via CYP3A4 inhibition. ⚠ Corrected 2026-10-05 (#830): the current label calls encorafenib a strong CYP3A4 inducer, so the expected direction is faster activation. The pair is unstudied either way (§5.5).
  • The absolute alcohol restriction, extending 14 days past the last dose, is a real quality-of-life and safety burden with genuine hidden-exposure hazards
  • ⚠ Her ctDNA is already undetectable — there is no headroom to show benefit, and confounding with active therapy compounds it. Risks are measurable; benefit is not.
  • She is in a deep, durable response (~11 months undetectable on encorafenib + binimetinib + cetuximab). Adding neuropathy and hepatotoxicity risk to a patient in remission is a different proposition from adding it to one who is progressing. (Corrected 2026-10-05: negative since 2025-12-05, about eight months when this review was written; see §8.)

My read: this is a defensible thing to try, provided the dose is confirmed, copper is decided deliberately, and neuro/hepatic monitoring is set up properly beforehand. The evidence is far thinner than the coverage implies — but the risk is bounded, detectable, and reversible, which is the actual argument in its favour.

What the corrected data changes is the framing, not the verdict. This is not “try it and watch the markers” — the markers are already at the floor. It is prophylaxis against recurrence, taken on mechanistic faith, with no available readout. That is a legitimate thing to choose; it just needs to be chosen deliberately rather than backed into. The practical consequence is that tolerability becomes the whole decision, which puts disproportionate weight on getting the dose right (§6) and on the baseline neuro exam (§5.3).

If I had to name the single highest-value output of this review: confirm the dose, and then ask the prescribing physician question 6 in §9 — what, concretely, would we expect to see if this is working?
