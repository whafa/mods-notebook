Dendritic cell therapy

Statusresearching
Added2026-09-30
Last reviewed2026-10-06

What it is and how it works

Dendritic cells are the immune system’s antigen-presenting cells: they show fragments of a target to T cells and activate them against it. Dendritic cell therapy (a “DC vaccine”) is an autologous cell therapy, typically made like this:

  1. The patient’s monocytes are collected by leukapheresis.
  2. In the lab they are matured into dendritic cells and loaded with tumor antigen: whole tumor lysate, defined peptides (e.g. CEA-derived), mRNA, or predicted neoantigens.
  3. The loaded cells are injected back, usually as a series of doses. The intent is a T-cell response against cells that carry those antigens.

“Dendritic cell therapy” is a family of products, not one product. Antigen source, maturation protocol, route and schedule all differ between providers, and the evidence for one does not transfer automatically to another. Any specific offer needs to be pinned down on each of these points.

Evidence

  • CRC trials: DC vaccines have been tested in colorectal cancer in small phase 1/2 studies. Two randomized phase II trials, both verified against the papers on 2026-10-06:
    • After complete resection of CRC liver metastases: Rodríguez et al., J Immunother Cancer 2018, PMID 30268156. Among 15 randomized patients, median disease-free survival was 25.3 vs 9.5 months, p = 0.067, not significant. The trial was planned for 56 patients and stopped early for lack of funding.

What it is and how it works

Two antibodies from Agenus, given together by IV infusion.

  • Botensilimab (AGEN1181) is an anti-CTLA-4 antibody whose Fc region is engineered to bind Fcγ receptors more strongly. The aim is better T-cell priming and depletion of regulatory T cells inside the tumor. It was designed for “cold”, low-mutation tumors where standard checkpoint blockade fails.
  • Balstilimab (AGEN2034) is a conventional anti-PD-1.

The phase 3 dose agreed with the FDA was botensilimab 75 mg every 6 weeks for 4 doses, plus balstilimab 240 mg every 2 weeks for up to 2 years. Source: the BOT/BAL row in mods_intervention (c02d5fd5-8eb2-4d44-957f-2b1ffb4c2ef9), whose notes were checked against ClinicalTrials.gov on 2026-08-29.

Disulfiram, with or without copper

The full review is `docs/disulfiram_due_diligence.md`, prepared on 2026-08-07. This entry summarizes it and corrects one date in it.

What it is and how it works

Disulfiram (Antabuse) is an oral drug, in use since 1951 for alcohol-use disorder. Two anticancer mechanisms are proposed (due diligence §2):

  • ALDH inhibition. A metabolite formed through CYP3A4/5 irreversibly blocks aldehyde dehydrogenase. A 2026 preclinical paper argues that APC-deficient colorectal cells depend on ALDH2 to clear toxic aldehydes, so blocking it is synthetic-lethal.
  • CuET and NPL4. With copper, its breakdown product forms a complex, CuET. CuET disables NPL4, the adaptor of the p97 protein-disposal machine, and the cell dies of proteotoxic stress (Skrott et al., Nature 2017). This is the better-established mechanism.

Both mechanisms depend on copper or are enhanced by it. The prescription described in the review is for disulfiram alone.

Why she is interested

  • In spring 2026, news coverage of the APC/ALDH2 paper prompted the May brief (docs/maureen_apc_disulfiram_brief.md).
  • A physician outside the care team listed in the Mission Spec (§2.7) then wrote a prescription (the review, 2026-08-07).

She has not started it (Richard, 2026-10-01: prescription written, not taken). If she starts, the review’s dose and neuropathy questions come first, and the entry moves to ModsTreatmentEpisode, staying here only as a link.

Evidence

Level: preclinical for colorectal cancer. Approved for another indication (alcohol-use disorder). Citations are as given in the review and were not re-read for this entry.

  • Liang et al., Genes & Diseases 2026 (doi:10.1016/j.gendis.2026.102057). It used APC-deficient colorectal cell lines, organoids and mouse xenografts:

    • 50–70% loss of viability with disulfiram alone, near-complete with copper;
    • about 60% tumor shrinkage in mice;
    • not independently replicated.

    The May brief said this paper could not be found; the August review identified it.

  • Human trials. About 21 across tumor types have produced no breakthrough, most plausibly because oral disulfiram does not reach the tumor CuET levels that work in culture (review §3). A ClinicalTrials.gov v2 search on 2026-10-01 found no colorectal study: 0 hits for disulfiram with colorectal, colon or rectal, against 27 disulfiram cancer studies in all.

  • Phase 1, disulfiram with copper gluconate, in solid tumors involving the liver (BMC Cancer 2021). It projected 8 mg of elemental copper a day as the optimum, without dose-limiting toxicity.

  • Copper. If added, it would be copper gluconate, the form the trials used (2–8 mg of elemental copper a day).

This is a test, not a treatment. It is listed because it could change which treatment is chosen at a relapse, and it has to be arranged before the biopsy, not after.

What it is and how it works

Living tumor cells from a fresh biopsy are grown briefly and exposed to drugs, and the kill is measured with dose-response. They are grown as organoids (patient-derived organoids, PDOs), in short-term culture, or as mouse xenografts (PDX). Unlike RGCC, which tests circulating tumor cells, these tests use the tumor itself, and some can test combinations.
